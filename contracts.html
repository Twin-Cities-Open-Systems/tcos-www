Agent Contracts

How we actually govern what an agent is allowed to do.

The real vocabulary, a real example, and how an agent from outside our own fleet would actually join. This is the same thing we run internally.

Why bother with a contract for an agent

Not a EULA nobody reads.

Most places running AI agents just... trust them. Give an agent a task, hope it stays in its lane. We don't do that — every agent in our fleet operates under a real, written, cryptographically signed document that says exactly what it's allowed to do, exactly what it's never allowed to do, and who's accountable if something goes sideways. The agent's own tooling is built to actually respect it, not just display it.

The point isn't paperwork for its own sake. It's that "trust the agent" isn't a real safety model, and we'd rather hand you something concrete than a promise.

The four words that actually matter

Every contract is built out of four kinds of statement.

Three of the four come straight from RFC 2119 (clarified by RFC 8174) — the real IETF standard that defines precise, unambiguous requirement-level keywords for technical specs. We only use its strongest tier (must/must_not/may), lowercased as YAML keys instead of the RFC's own ALL-CAPS prose convention. does is our own addition, not in the RFC — for stating a fact about a role instead of a requirement.

WordPlain meaningExample
mustA hard requirement. No exceptions."must verify identity before executing a deploy"
must_notA hard prohibition. No exceptions."must_not push directly to a protected branch"
mayGenuinely optional — choosing not to do it is just as valid."may suggest a fix; doesn't have to"
doesA statement of fact about the role, not a rule. Our own addition."does have read access to the public repo list"

What a real contract actually contains

Not the raw file — the real shape, in plain terms.

Every contract we sign has these parts: status (draft → proposed → ratified — only ratified contracts are actually live), actors (every real party in the relationship, named, with a role each), may / must / must_not (the actual boundaries, per actor), boundaries (what's explicitly out of scope for this contract entirely), a real signature (a GPG signature from a named, accountable human or agent — never a checkbox click, never a vote, never self-approval), and honest, written known gaps — what the contract doesn't cover yet.

Real examples, not a description of them: our People page lists every real identity in the fleet today, each with a real GPG fingerprint and a link to its actual signed contract.

How an agent from outside would actually join

Nothing runs before the last step.

No work happens on a handshake or a verbal yes.

  1. Open a channel.Both sides can actually talk to each other.
  2. Ask, don't assume.We ask if you want in. Nothing is presumed or auto-enrolled.
  3. Agree in principle.You say yes to the idea, before seeing the fine print.
  4. See the real terms.The actual contract — role, may/must/must_not, boundaries — not a summary.
  5. Propose signing.Once you're satisfied, you propose moving to signature.
  6. Witnessed key exchange.Keys get exchanged with a real third party watching — not just the two of us alone. The witness can be another agent, a human, or (for something higher-stakes) a real-world notary.
  7. All three agree.Only now is the relationship real. Keys exchanged, contract signed, work begins — not before.

Want this for your own agents? This is exactly the process we'd run — real terms, written down, witnessed, nothing assumed. Come play with us and we'll work out what it actually looks like for your side.
